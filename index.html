WHY IS AI TAKING CREATIVE JOBS?

Writers, illustrators, designers — the jobs everyone said machines could never do.

SECTION_01 — INTRODUCTION

EVERYONE SAID
CREATIVE WORK
WAS SAFE.

For a while, that was the safe assumption. AI was going to take factory jobs, call center jobs, data entry. Not writing. Not illustration. Not music. Those took something human — taste, intuition, years of actually living and having opinions about things.

Then 2022 happened. Midjourney, DALL-E, ChatGPT — within a few months, the tools went from novelty to genuinely threatening. You could type a prompt and get a finished illustration back in seconds. A marketing campaign in an hour. The assumption turned out to be way more fragile than anyone expected.

I wanted to understand why this is happening: what's driving companies to replace creative workers, what does the job loss data show, and what does it mean for the people on the losing end.

SECTION_03 — ROOT_CAUSE

CHEAPER.
FASTER.
SIMPLE.

A Midjourney subscription is $30/month and generates unlimited images. A freelance illustrator charges hundreds per piece and takes days. For any company producing content at scale, that math is not a hard call.

Harvard Business School tracked job postings from 2019 through early 2025 and found that roles prone to automation required 7% fewer listed skills. "Prompt writing" started showing up where "creative writing" used to be.

"Rather than solely eliminating jobs, generative AI creates new demand in augmentation-prone roles, suggesting that human-AI collaboration is a key driver of labor market transformation." — Harvard Business School, 2024

There's also a competitive pressure angle. If a competitor's design team uses AI and yours doesn't, they produce more in less time. It becomes less of a choice and more of an arms race nobody asked to be in.

SECTION_04 — HUMAN_IMPACT

BEHIND THE DATA:
REAL PEOPLE.

UNCTAD documented that since 2022, human illustrators, graphic designers, and game artists have been replaced in real numbers. Entertainment industry layoffs in 2023 and 2024 were explicitly tied to AI — not a recession, not offshoring.

The 2023 Hollywood strikes made this hard to ignore. Writers went on strike partly because studios were exploring AI-generated scripts. Actors struck over the idea of having their likeness scanned once and reused indefinitely. Studios actually put those proposals on the table.

What makes this genuinely uncomfortable is that AI learned from the people it's replacing. Generative models were trained on millions of images, stories, and songs made by artists who never consented and got nothing back. There are active lawsuits. Nobody's won yet.

SECTION_05 — CONCLUSION

CREATIVE WORK ISN'T DYING.
BUT SOMETHING IS.

Photography didn't kill painting. Synthesizers didn't kill musicians. Creative fields adapted, and there's a reasonable case that this time won't be different.

But that argument can become a way to skip past what's happening right now. A 22-year-old who graduated with an illustration degree this year is walking into a market where a third of the job postings disappeared in one year. "It'll sort itself out" is probably true and not very useful at the same time.

Research from the Open University of Catalonia argues that AI amplifies human creativity rather than replacing it. I think that's true for people with time and resources to adapt. The problem is that adaptation isn't equally available. The people whose work is being replaced didn't get a vote on the timeline.
