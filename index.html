WHY IS AI TAKING CREATIVE JOBS?

Writers, illustrators, designers — the jobs everyone said machines could never do.

SECTION_01 — INTRODUCTION

EVERYONE SAID
CREATIVE WORK
WAS SAFE.

For many years, people assumed creative work was safe. It was known that automation was going to take over roles in customer service, industrial, and data related industries but not the people who wrote ads or created designs. That work felt different and humanely created. It relied on taste, context, and a real understanding of what connects people.

Then 2022 happened. Tools such as Midjourney and ChatGPT slowly entered their way to creative markets. Within a few months, companies used these tools to generate visuals, scripts, and automate their marketing workflow without having a human to do it. The barrier everyone assumed existed turned out to be way lower than expected.

This project goes beyond the common conversation about the interruption AI caused. It actually digs deeper: why companies are moving rapidly to replace creative roles, what the data reflects when you analyse it closely, and what it all means for the people whose work is on the line.

ACCELERATING.

This is not about the layoffs. What happened is even more silent than that. Companies aren't cutting people they already have employed — there just isn't an open job market for new roles. The hiring process is disappearing. For example, open roles for graphic artists dropped 12% in 2024, and then continued to drop another 33% in 2025. It's clear, there's no recession that is driving this. Data from Lunt's analysis of 180 million job listings points to something more gradual but more significant. One by one every company is deciding they no longer need to hire humans to make creatives — they can simply purchase a tool to do it for them.

SECTION_03 — ROOT_CAUSE

CHEAPER.
FASTER.
SIMPLE.

A Midjourney subscription costs approximately $30/month and can generate unlimited visuals. On the other hand, freelance illustrators charge hundreds of dollars per piece and take extra time. For companies, the smartest idea would be to pick low cost high return and that would be using AI. And it's no longer just images. Many new platforms such as Higgsfield AI let a single creator produce full cinematic campaigns — work that once required a whole production crew but now can be done with a subscription.

SECTION_04 — HUMAN_IMPACT

BEHIND THE DATA:
REAL PEOPLE.

UNCTAD tracked real job losses — illustrators, graphic designers, game designers since 2022. These weren't distant statistics. They were real people whose work disappeared not because of a market crash but because the industry quietly moved on without them.

The Hollywood strikes made that transformation visible. Writers went on strike as studios began using AI-generated scripts. Actors did the same. These concerns actually showed up in actual contract negotiations.

There's also a harder reality underneath it all: all the models replacing this work were trained on the work of the same people being replaced. Millions of images, stories, and songs were used without consent or any compensation. Lawsuits were ongoing, but no results have come out of them yet.

Maguire argues that disruption in creative industries isn't new. The printing press, the camera — each was expected to replace creative work, but it didn't. But this moment just feels different. This tool isn't just an assistant, it's the main character. It produces output that can be unique from human work.

SECTION_05 — COUNTERARGUMENT

BUT WAIT —
NEW ROLES.

The other side of this isn't nothing. The World Economic Forum's 2023 analysis points out that gen AI is creating new roles at the same time it's replacing old ones, such as AI prompt engineers and editors. Those jobs are real, and people are getting paid to do them.

Research from Harvard University (Srinivasan et al.) describes "augmentation-prone" roles as those which AI is able to automate human output instead of completely replacing it. A designer who knows tools like Midjourney can now produce 10x output in a day compared to what would be created in a week. The best gets faster and more valuable. The middle starts to fall.

Both things are happening at the same time. AI is opening doors for some creative workers and closing them for others. Whether it's net positive depends entirely on where you sit in the industry — and whether you had time to adapt before the floor dropped out.

The problem with the optimistic take is the disconnect between what is being lost and what's being created. A script writer who has spent fifteen years building a career for themselves doesn't just become an AI content strategist overnight. The skills don't match cleanly, and the transition is near impossible to happen fast enough. It's not just that the path is harder — but for many it disappeared completely.

"

Generative AI creates new demand in augmentation-prone roles — but whether displaced workers can access those roles is a completely different question.

SECTION_06 — CONCLUSION

SO WHERE
DOES THAT
LEAVE US?

The short answer is: somewhere uncomfortable. AI isn't replacing creative workers because it's better at creativity — it's replacing them because it's faster, better, and cheaper.

Bloomberg, UNCTAD, Harvard University, and the World Economic Forum all point in the same direction — the middle. The working professionals who built stable careers on skills that now have a cheaper alternative.

Generative AI was trained on human creative work without clear permission or compensation. The lawsuits are moving through the courts but there is no clean verdict yet. In the meantime, models are being consistently trained on more human output, and quietly "murdering" more jobs.

In 2024, UNCTAD warned people that if AI makes it no longer financially worthwhile for people to spend years developing creative skills, then fewer people will invest in becoming high level creators in the first place. Generative AI depends on human creativity, even as it makes it harder to sustain. That's not an infrastructure that resolves on its own over time.

What happens next has less to do with the technology, and more to do with the decisions around it — whether studios, platforms, legislators and courts decide if creative work is valuable enough to be protected. So far, they haven't, and until they do nothing about this changes.
